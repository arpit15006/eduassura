## The Same Achievement, Typed Five Times

A faculty member publishes a journal paper. Over the next year, that one paper is typed into a department newsletter, an annual report, a quarterly data sheet, an appraisal form and an accreditation file. Each copy is keyed in by a different person, from a different source, at a different time.

By the time anyone checks, there are five versions. One has a shortened title, one is missing the DOI, one lists the wrong co-author order and one sits in the wrong quarter. Nobody is sure which version is correct, and the faculty member who could settle it was never asked.

This is not a people problem. It is a process problem. When data is re-entered at every stage, errors are introduced at every stage.

## What Verification at the Source Means

The fix is a simple principle with three parts:

1. **Enter once**: The person who did the work records it once, with proof attached, while the details are fresh.
2. **Verify once**: Someone accountable checks that record against its proof and marks it Verified, returns it for correction, or rejects it with a reason.
3. **Report everywhere**: Every downstream output reads from that same verified record instead of a fresh copy.

If a correction is ever needed, it is made on the original record, and every report picks it up automatically.

## Why Checking at the End Does Not Work

Many quality cells try to clean data just before a submission. It feels efficient, but it fails for predictable reasons.

### The Context Is Gone

At the end of a quarter, a coordinator reviewing a full quarter of publications and events cannot chase every author for a missing certificate. The people who could answer quickly have moved on to the next semester.

### Corrections Do Not Flow Back

When someone fixes a typo in the final spreadsheet, the fix lives only in that spreadsheet. Next year, the same error returns from the same source.

### Nobody Owns the Record

If five people typed the same achievement, who is responsible for it being right? In practice, nobody is.

## Who Should Verify What

Verification works best when responsibilities are explicit:

- **Faculty** own the accuracy of their entries and the proof they upload.
- **Heads of Department** verify selected items that sit closest to departmental work, such as portfolios and event proposals.
- **The quality cell** verifies records against proof and returns them for correction with a clear note when something is missing.
- **Leadership** uses verified data for decisions and never re-keys it.

## Capture Everything a Report Will Need

A record can only be reused if it was complete to begin with. The rule of thumb: if any report will ever need a field, capture it at entry.

For a research publication, that usually means:

- Title, authors and co-authors
- Journal or conference, with indexing details
- DOI, ISSN or ISBN
- Date of publication and the quarter it belongs to
- Funding source, if any
- Relevant Sustainable Development Goals
- The proof itself

It feels like more work up front. It is far less work than rebuilding the same details from memory six months later.

## Status Is Part of the Data

Every record should carry a visible status: Pending, Verified, Correction or Rejected. That status matters as much as the content.

- Reports and scores should count only Verified records.
- Dashboards should show Pending and Correction counts so the backlog is visible early, not on the last day.
- Rejections should always carry a reason, so faculty learn what is acceptable next time.

## Getting Started Without a Big Bang

You do not need to change everything at once:

1. **Pick one module and one quarter**: Research publications are a good first choice because they are frequent and well defined.
2. **Agree on acceptable proof**: Write it down before the quarter starts.
3. **Stop parallel channels**: For that module, stop accepting entries by email or shared spreadsheet.
4. **Review the returns**: Look at why records were sent back for correction, then improve the form or the guidance.

## Conclusion

Quality data is not cleaned at the end. It is created correctly at the beginning. When every achievement is entered once by the person who did the work, verified once by someone accountable, and reused everywhere, the quality cell stops reconciling spreadsheets and starts improving quality.

Every achievement, on the record, once.
