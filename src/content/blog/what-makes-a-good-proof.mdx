## Why the Proof Matters More Than the Entry

During an audit or accreditation visit, nobody questions the form. They question the evidence. A record without convincing proof is, for practical purposes, a record that does not exist.

Good proof also makes verification faster. When the document clearly supports the entry, the quality cell can verify it in seconds instead of sending it back.

## The Four Tests of a Good Proof

Before uploading, check that the document is:

1. **Relevant**: It proves this specific activity, not a related one.
2. **Complete**: It shows the name, the date and the outcome together.
3. **Legible**: Text, seals and signatures can be read without zooming and guessing.
4. **Attributable**: It clearly links the activity to the faculty member or student claiming it.

If a document fails any one of these, it will probably be returned for correction.

## What to Upload for Common Record Types

### Research Publications

The published first page showing the title, author names, journal and volume details is usually the strongest proof. An acceptance letter is useful while the paper is in press, but replace it once published. Indexing claims should be supported, not just stated.

### Events and Activities

A sanctioned proposal or circular, the attendance record, and a short report with a few photographs. Photographs alone rarely prove who organised what.

### Patents and Intellectual Property

The filing receipt or publication record with the application number, and the grant certificate once issued. Keep each stage as it happens.

### Consultancy and Research Projects

The sanction or work order showing the funding body, amount and duration, along with completion or utilisation documents at the end.

### Guest Lectures and External Contributions

An invitation letter or certificate from the host institution that names the speaker, topic and date.

### Awards and Student Achievements

The certificate or official result, showing the name, the event and the level of the award.

### MOUs

The signed document, with the dates of signing and validity visible, and a record of activities carried out under it.

## File Habits That Save Hours Later

- **One record, one proof set**: Do not reuse a combined PDF across several entries.
- **Use PDF for documents**: Scan at a readable resolution, and avoid photographs of screens.
- **Name files meaningfully**: Something like the record type, your name and the date is enough.
- **Upload at the time of entry**: Proof hunted down months later is often the wrong version.
- **Share only what is needed**: Redact personal details that are not relevant to the claim.

## A Checklist for Verifiers

Quality cell members can use a quick routine for each record:

- Does the name on the proof match the person claiming it?
- Does the date fall in the quarter being reported?
- Do the key fields on the form match the document exactly?
- Is anything claimed on the form missing from the proof?

If the answer to the last question is yes, return the record with a note that says exactly what to add.

## When the Auditor Asks

The real test comes when a reviewer asks to see the evidence for every publication from a department in a given year. If proofs live in personal folders and email threads, that request takes days.

## Conclusion

Audit readiness is not a project that starts a month before a visit. It is the result of small habits repeated with every entry: the right document, complete and legible, uploaded at the time the work is recorded. Build those habits into the workflow, and the audit becomes a download.
