## Why SDG Mapping Keeps Coming Up

Universities are increasingly asked to show not just how much research they produce, but what it contributes to society. The seventeen Sustainable Development Goals give a shared vocabulary for that conversation, and quality cells are regularly asked to report work goal by goal.

The difficulty is that most institutions try to map their work to the SDGs after the fact, often in the week before a report is due. The result is rushed, inconsistent and hard to defend.

## Tag at the Source, Not at Report Time

The person best placed to say which goals a piece of work addresses is the person who did it. When faculty tag the relevant SDGs while entering a publication or project, three things improve:

- **Accuracy**: The tag reflects what the work actually does.
- **Consistency**: Everyone uses the same list and the same guidance.
- **Reviewability**: The quality cell can check the tag during normal verification.

## What Can Be Tagged

SDG tagging is most useful when it goes beyond publications. Consider tagging:

- Research publications
- External research projects and seed money projects
- Consultancy projects
- Patents and other intellectual property
- Events, workshops and outreach activities
- Student club and chapter activities

Together, these show both research contribution and on-the-ground engagement.

## A Simple Tagging Rule

Over-tagging is the most common mistake. A paper that mentions sustainability once in its introduction does not address SDG 13. A practical rule:

1. **Tag a goal only if the work directly addresses it**: A reader of the abstract or summary should agree with the tag without being persuaded.
2. **Prefer one or two goals**: Most work has a clear primary goal. Add a second only if it is genuinely central.
3. **When unsure, leave it out**: An untagged record is better than a misleading one.

## Illustrative Examples

These fictional examples show the kind of reasoning to encourage:

- **A study on low-cost water filtration for rural households** fits SDG 6, Clean Water and Sanitation.
- **A consultancy project on energy audits for small manufacturers** fits SDG 7, Affordable and Clean Energy, and possibly SDG 9, Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure.
- **A faculty development programme on inclusive teaching** fits SDG 4, Quality Education.
- **A paper on traffic flow modelling for a mid-sized city** fits SDG 11, Sustainable Cities and Communities.
- **An MOU-based community health camp run by students** fits SDG 3, Good Health and Well-being, and SDG 17, Partnerships for the Goals.

## Verifying SDG Tags

Tags should be verified like any other field. During verification, the quality cell checks whether the tag is plausible given the title and summary. If it is not, the record goes back for correction with a short note, for example: "Please review the SDG tag. The work addresses water quality rather than climate action."

Over a few quarters, these notes teach faculty what good tagging looks like far better than a policy document.

## Reviewing Work Goal by Goal

Once tags are in place and verified, the SDG-wise view becomes a planning tool, not just a report:

### Where Is the Work Concentrated?

Most institutions find a few goals with dense activity and several with very little. That is useful information, not a failure.

### What Kind of Work Sits Under Each Goal?

A goal backed only by publications is different from one backed by funded projects, consultancy and community events. The mix shows depth.

### Which Departments Contribute?

Department-wise distribution often reveals collaboration opportunities, for example civil engineering and management both working on urban mobility.

## Conclusion

SDG mapping is only credible when it is honest and consistent. Tag at the source, keep the rule simple, verify tags like any other data, and the SDG-wise picture of your university will be one you can stand behind in any review.
